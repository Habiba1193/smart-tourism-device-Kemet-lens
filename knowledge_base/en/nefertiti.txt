SHORT DESCRIPTION
Nefertiti was a powerful queen of ancient Egypt and the Great Royal Wife of Pharaoh Akhenaten during the 18th Dynasty. She is most famous today because of her painted limestone bust, discovered at Amarna in 1912 and now displayed in Berlin’s Neues Museum.

OVERVIEW
Nefertiti lived during the New Kingdom, in the 18th Dynasty, and was one of the most important royal women in ancient Egyptian history. She was the wife of Akhenaten, the pharaoh who promoted the worship of Aten, the sun disk, and moved the royal capital to Akhetaten, modern Tell el-Amarna. Nefertiti appears in royal art not only as a queen, but also as an active religious and political figure beside the king.

Her name is usually translated as “The Beautiful One Has Come.” She became a symbol of beauty, queenship, and power because of the famous bust made during the Amarna Period. The bust shows her with a tall blue crown, elegant neck, broad collar, and idealized facial features.

HISTORICAL BACKGROUND
Nefertiti lived during one of the most unusual periods in Egyptian history. Her husband Akhenaten changed Egyptian religion by promoting the worship of Aten over the traditional state god Amun and many older deities. This religious and political change is called the Amarna Period.

Akhenaten founded a new capital city called Akhetaten, now known as Tell el-Amarna. Nefertiti appears in many Amarna artworks with Akhenaten and their daughters under the rays of Aten. These scenes present the royal family as central to worship and show a more intimate artistic style than earlier Egyptian royal art.

ERA

DYNASTY

APPROXIMATE DATE
14th century BCE, especially during the reign of Akhenaten, commonly dated around 1353–1336 BCE.

FAMOUS FOR
Nefertiti is famous for being the Great Royal Wife of Akhenaten, her major role in the Amarna Period, her association with Aten worship, her elegant painted bust, and her image as a symbol of ancient Egyptian beauty and royal power.

LOCATION
Historically, Nefertiti is strongly connected with Tell el-Amarna / Akhetaten, the capital city founded by Akhenaten. The famous bust of Nefertiti was discovered at Tell el-Amarna in 1912 and is displayed today in the Neues Museum in Berlin.

THE FAMOUS BUST OF NEFERTITI
The bust of Nefertiti is one of the most famous works of ancient Egyptian art. It was found in the workshop of the sculptor Thutmose at Amarna. The object is especially important because it shows the refined style of Amarna royal art: elegant, realistic-looking, and idealized at the same time.

Important features of the bust include the tall blue crown, the uraeus cobra, the long neck, the painted face, the broad collar, and the missing left eye inlay. The missing eye is one of the most discussed details of the bust.

ICONOGRAPHY AND APPEARANCE
Nefertiti is usually shown with royal symbols that emphasize her elevated status. She may appear wearing a tall blue crown, a broad decorative collar, fine royal clothing, and a uraeus cobra on the forehead. Amarna-style art often gives her a long neck, elegant face, and soft facial modeling.

ROLE IN RELIGION
Nefertiti played a major role in the religious life of the Amarna Period. Akhenaten’s religion focused on Aten, the visible disk of the sun. In many images, Aten’s rays end in small hands reaching toward the royal family, showing divine blessing and life.

ROLE IN POLITICS AND POWER
Nefertiti’s exact political authority is debated, but she clearly had an unusually visible position. Her repeated appearance in royal and religious art suggests strong public importance. Some scholars have suggested that she may have ruled as a co-regent or may have been connected with the mysterious ruler Neferneferuaten after Akhenaten, but this remains debated.

FAMILY
Nefertiti was the Great Royal Wife of Akhenaten. She and Akhenaten are commonly associated with six daughters: Meritaten, Meketaten, Ankhesenpaaten / Ankhesenamun, Neferneferuaten Tasherit, Neferneferure, and Setepenre. Her exact parentage is uncertain.

CONNECTION TO TUTANKHAMUN
Nefertiti is connected to Tutankhamun through the royal family of the Amarna Period. Tutankhamun later married Ankhesenamun, one of Akhenaten and Nefertiti’s daughters. However, Nefertiti herself is not securely proven to be Tutankhamun’s mother.

SYMBOLISM
Nefertiti symbolizes royal beauty, queenship, feminine power, religious change during the Amarna Period, Aten worship, solar imagery, Amarna art, political influence of royal women, and mystery because her later life and tomb remain uncertain.

MATERIALS ASSOCIATED WITH ARTIFACTS
Nefertiti-related objects and images may be associated with limestone, painted stucco, sandstone, pigments, faience, glass, gold, and decorative inlays.

WHY IT MATTERS
Nefertiti matters because she represents one of the strongest examples of royal female visibility in ancient Egypt. Her images show how queens could participate in religion, politics, royal family ideology, and public symbolism. Her bust became one of the most famous images of ancient Egyptian art and helped shape the modern world’s visual idea of Egyptian beauty.

HISTORICAL TIMELINE
- 14th century BCE – Nefertiti lives during the 18th Dynasty.
- Reign of Akhenaten – Nefertiti becomes Great Royal Wife.
- Year 5 of Akhenaten’s reign – Akhenaten establishes Akhetaten / Amarna as a new royal capital.
- Amarna Period – Nefertiti appears prominently in religious and royal art.
- Later Amarna Period – Her final years become unclear in the historical record.
- 1912 – The famous bust is discovered at Tell el-Amarna.
- Modern period – The bust becomes one of the most famous Egyptian artifacts in the world.

DID YOU KNOW?
Nefertiti’s bust is famous partly because it looks extremely complete and lifelike, but the left eye inlay is missing.

HIDDEN DETAIL
The tall blue crown on Nefertiti’s bust is strongly associated with her image. It helps identify her and makes the bust instantly recognizable.

FUN FACTS
- Nefertiti’s name means “The Beautiful One Has Come.”
- She was the Great Royal Wife of Akhenaten.
- She lived during the Amarna Period.
- Her famous bust was discovered in 1912.
- Her left eye inlay is missing on the famous bust.

- Her final years and burial place remain uncertain.
