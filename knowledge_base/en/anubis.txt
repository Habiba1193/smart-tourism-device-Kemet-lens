SHORT DESCRIPTION
Anubis was an ancient Egyptian god connected with mummification, funerary rituals, protection of the dead, tomb guardianship, and the journey to the afterlife. He is usually shown as a black jackal or as a man with a jackal head.

OVERVIEW
Anubis was one of the most important funerary deities in ancient Egypt. His image appears in tombs, funerary texts, burial objects, statues, and scenes connected with death and the afterlife. Egyptians believed that Anubis protected the dead, guarded tombs, guided souls, and helped with the rituals of mummification.

His jackal form may be connected with desert animals seen near cemeteries. Instead of seeing the jackal only as a threat to graves, Egyptian religion transformed the image into a protective divine figure watching over the dead.

HISTORICAL BACKGROUND
In early Egyptian religion, Anubis had a major role as a lord and protector of the dead. During the Early Dynastic and Old Kingdom periods, he was especially important in funerary belief. Later, Osiris became more central as ruler of the afterlife, but Anubis remained essential as the god of embalming, tomb protection, and judgment scenes.

Priests performing embalming rituals could wear a jackal mask to represent Anubis during mummification. This showed that Anubis was not only a mythological figure, but also part of real funerary practice.

ERA
Ancient Egyptian religion across many periods

DYNASTY
Not limited to one dynasty; Anubis was worshiped throughout ancient Egyptian history.

APPROXIMATE DATE
Anubis appears in ancient Egyptian religion from early periods and remained important through later Egyptian history.

FAMOUS FOR
Anubis is famous for his black jackal-headed form, mummification and embalming, protecting tombs and mummies, guiding souls to the afterlife, the Weighing of the Heart judgment scene, and guarding the necropolis.

LOCATION
Images and statues of Anubis are found in tombs, temples, funerary objects, burial scenes, and museum collections connected with ancient Egypt. Anubis was strongly associated with necropolises, tomb entrances, burial chambers, and funerary shrines.

IDENTITY AND ROLES
Anubis had several important roles in Egyptian religion:
- God of mummification: connected with embalming and preserving the body.
- Protector of tombs: guarded graves, mummies, and burial places.
- Guide of souls: helped the dead travel safely to the afterlife.
- Judge assistant: supervised the weighing of the heart.
- Guardian of the necropolis: watched over cemeteries and sacred burial lands.
- Funerary deity: appeared in burial rituals, tomb art, and funerary texts.

EPITHETS AND TITLES
Anubis had several titles that explained his religious functions:
- Lord of the Sacred Land: connected to the necropolis and burial ground.
- He Who Is upon His Mountain: guardian watching over cemeteries.
- Foremost of the Westerners: linked with the west, the land of the dead.
- He Who Is in the Place of Embalming: connected with mummification and embalming.

MYTHIC CONTEXT AND FAMILY
Anubis’s family background changes depending on the myth or period. In some traditions, he is described as the son of Nephthys and Osiris. In other traditions, he may be connected with Ra. These differences are normal in ancient Egyptian religion because myths could vary by region, temple, and time period.

His female counterpart is often named Anput, and his daughter is often named Kebechet, associated with purification and cooling or refreshing the deceased.

ICONOGRAPHY AND APPEARANCE
Anubis is usually shown in two main forms:
- Full jackal: a black jackal lying down or standing, often guarding a tomb or shrine.
- Jackal-headed man: a human male body with the head of a black jackal.

He is often shown with a black body or black jackal head. This color is symbolic, not naturalistic. It represents the color of mummified flesh and the fertile black soil of the Nile, linking death with regeneration and rebirth.

WHY ANUBIS HAS A JACKAL HEAD
Jackals and wild dogs were often seen near desert edges and cemeteries. Because burial areas were vulnerable, the Egyptians transformed this animal association into a protective symbol. Anubis’s jackal form made him a powerful guardian of graves rather than a destroyer of them.

WHY ANUBIS IS BLACK
Anubis is usually black because black symbolized mummified flesh, the fertile soil of the Nile, regeneration, rebirth, protection in death, and transformation into the afterlife. The black color does not mean evil. In Egyptian symbolism, it was strongly connected with renewal and life after death.

RITUAL FUNCTIONS
Anubis had major roles in funerary rituals.

Mummification and Embalming:
Anubis was associated with embalming and the preservation of the body. He was believed to protect the mummy and help prepare the deceased for the afterlife. Priests involved in embalming could wear jackal masks to represent Anubis during rituals.

Weighing of the Heart:
In the judgment scene, Anubis supervises or performs the weighing of the deceased person’s heart against the feather of Ma’at, the symbol of truth, balance, and justice. If the heart was balanced, the person could continue into the afterlife. If not, the monster Ammit could devour the heart.

Guide of Souls:
Anubis also acted as a guide for the dead, helping the soul pass safely through the dangerous transition from life to the afterlife.

THE WEIGHING OF THE HEART SCENE
The Weighing of the Heart is one of the most famous scenes connected with Anubis. Anubis guides the deceased and weighs the heart. Ma’at’s feather represents truth and balance. Thoth records the result. Osiris presides over the judgment. Ammit devours the unworthy heart.

CULT AND WORSHIP
Anubis was worshiped widely in ancient Egypt. He was associated with places such as Cynopolis, meaning “City of the Dog,” and other cult or necropolis centers. His protection could be invoked through shrines, tomb seals, statues, and funerary imagery.

MATERIALS ASSOCIATED WITH ANUBIS ARTIFACTS
Anubis statues and images may be associated with wood, gesso, paint, gold leaf, stone, limestone, granite, and inlay. Wood with gesso, gilding, and paint was common for movable funerary shrine figures, while stone was used for statues and reliefs.

PLACEMENT AND ORIENTATION
Anubis figures could be placed near tomb entrances, thresholds, on top of shrines, near sarcophagi, near canopic equipment, in burial chambers, and in funerary scenes painted on tomb walls.

SYMBOLISM
Anubis symbolizes mummification, protection, tomb guardianship, death and transition, judgment, safe passage to the afterlife, regeneration, rebirth, preservation of the body, and divine guidance.

WHY IT MATTERS
Anubis matters because he helps explain ancient Egyptian beliefs about death and the afterlife. His role shows that death was not viewed only as an ending, but as a carefully guided journey requiring rituals, protection, judgment, and divine support.

HISTORICAL TIMELINE
- Early Egyptian history – Anubis appears as a major funerary god.
- Old Kingdom – Anubis holds an important role connected with the dead.
- Middle Kingdom – Osiris becomes more central as ruler of the afterlife, while Anubis remains important.
- New Kingdom – Anubis appears widely in tomb paintings, funerary texts, and burial objects.
- Tutankhamun’s tomb – Anubis shrine shows him as a protective jackal figure.
- Later periods – Anubis remains an important symbol of mummification and afterlife protection.
- Modern period – Anubis becomes one of the most recognizable Egyptian gods.
